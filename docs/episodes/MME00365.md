> *"They are the tools of a dangerous trade employed by men and women who risk their lives to bear witness to war. Now, Frontline reporting on Modern Marvels. In March 2003, a military convoy rolled across the desert in Iraq, the first wave of a ground attack by the United States against the regime of Saddam Hussein. But along with soldiers and their weapons of war, the convoy carried civilian correspondence in the vanguard of another revolution. Equipped with satellite phones, video phones, and portable uplinks, these frontline reporters could transmit the sounds and sights of war in ways never previously employed on a battlefield. Covering war, like waging war, has always depended on speed and flexibility. For the embedded reporters, equipment light and compact enough to keep pace with the troops made it possible to communicate in real time from moving battle zones. If it was time to leave, it was time to leave, and there would be no waiting around for the TV people to gather all our things. That put us under a little bit of a constriction and a time limitation, because we didn't know when they were just going to pick up and leave. Sam Sambatero was part of a TV crew whose equipment included a portable satellite uplink, which could be set up or disassembled like an umbrella within 20 minutes for live broadcast. We were able to shoot our video, capture it onto the laptops, edit it if we needed to, and send those compressed files back on the satellites. When our colonel would decide where we were going to overnight, I would set the dish up, find the satellite, get power from the military, and away we would go. To broadcast even more quickly, TV crews also used a video phone known as the Talking Head. Its choppy low bandwidth signal could be transmitted by one or two satellite telephones with portable flat-screen antennas."*

</details>

### 🎙️ Show Introduction

> **Opening Hook:**  
> *"Now, containers on Modern Marvels."*

> They hold just about everything we need, from condiments to cargo. Made of steel, aluminum, paper, and glass. They protect and preserve. They're underground and above ground, and they journey around the world. They've revolutionized civilization. Now, containers on Modern Marvels. In China, TVs, blue jeans, and light bulbs are being manufactured by the millions. But how do these goods get to stores in the United States? And the transport industry's ubiquitous standard shipping containers? To move that enormous bulk from the offshore location of the mainland, they need many, many big boxes. And the big boxes we speak of are those 45-foot containers that are traveling down the road with the blue jeans or the televisions or the tires that you see stacked in the shelves in your retail outlets. The container's journey from source to store is a long one. Typically, the goods are stuffed and manufactured in a distribution center somewhere in China. In this case, we'll say the Guangdong province. Then from Guangdong to the port of Hong Kong, for example, that transport is done by one of three ways. One, over the road via truck. Two, via rail. Or three, via smaller vessels through a river. Then it arrives to the Hong Kong port. Typically between 4,000 containers to about 9,000 for the bigger ships are loaded at the Hong Kong port. Then the vessel leaves Hong Kong to the port of L.A., for example. Once arriving at the port of L.A., the container is unloaded. At that point, the container is loaded onto either a rail or another truck and then transported to its final destination. The container will make about 120 trips from port to port in its approximate 10-year life. On the journey from Far East to the mainland during typhoon season, there could potentially be 40- and 50-foot waves that rock the vessel itself to very severe pitched angles.

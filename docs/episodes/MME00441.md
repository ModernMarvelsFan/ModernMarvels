### 🎙️ Show Introduction

> **Opening Hook:**  
> *"Now, The Butcher on Modern Marvels."*

> The libertarians need not apply. The 21st century butcher has many faces. A grim reaper, a carcass processor, a meat cutter, a safety inspector, and a salesman. He turns livestock into steak, hamburger, and sausage. He even makes house calls. Now, The Butcher on Modern Marvels. Each year in the United States, massive feedlots schedule more than 135 million head of livestock for their appointments with the butcher. The lone multitasking butcher of old is nearly extinct. In his place is an army of high-speed cutters and bacteria control specialists at a mass production factory. But in a world of prepackaged products, there's a growing movement to recapture the secrets of the old world butcher. A little over a pound and a half. At the swank Nick and Steph's Steakhouse in downtown Los Angeles, patrons come to celebrate a forgotten art from the butcher's meat locker. It's called dry-aged beef. Nick and Steps imports whole strip loins and ages them in a climate-controlled aging room for up to 21 days. What's happening is it promotes the enzymes within the beef to start breaking down the tendons and muscle fiber. In essence, what it's doing is reversing the process of the rigor mortis, so it's actually loosening up the meat and tenderizing it. Some dry-aged steaks sell for as high as $50 per 10-ounce cut. Their high cost is a direct result of shrinkage. As the beef ages, the outer surface decays and surface bacteria must be removed. Extracting the stripped steaks and ribeyes requires a skilled butcher. As you can see, it's completely broken down, high marbleization, completely tenderized,

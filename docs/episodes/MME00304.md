> The following program was produced before the December 26, 2004 earthquake and tsunami struck South Asia. They're among the deadliest of natural disasters. Vicious sea predators that have taken tens of thousands of lives in the past century alone. They strike with lethal force, decimating everything in their way. Today, scientists are beginning to understand these killer waves. Computerized guoys, wave machines, satellites, and 3D models are helping to predict when and where the next deadly tsunami will hit. It's a technological race against nature. Nature Tech Tsunamis, next on Modern Marvels. At the bottom of the sea lurks a beast like no other. Tsunamis, the Japanese word for harbor waves, appear without warning. They are triggered by seismic events, such as earthquakes and volcanoes, that generate a displacement of the seafloor and massive underwater waves. These waves travel undetected across the ocean at jet speed. When they reach land, the waves slow down, but not before they've grown to terrifying heights, towering walls of water that can devour entire cities. Tsunamis are among the most misunderstood of all natural disasters. Unlike many other natural disasters, like hurricanes, you know that something's coming because the wind starts to blow really fast or it starts to rain really hard. There are no real natural signs in advance that a tsunami's coming until the water actually starts to move. For centuries, tsunamis have puzzled scientists because few people who have actually witnessed the disaster have lived to tell about it.

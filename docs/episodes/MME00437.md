### 🎙️ Show Introduction

> **Opening Hook:**  
> *"Now, more Nature's Engineers on Modern Marvels."*

> They aren't human, but they use tools, build intricate structures, devise diabolical traps to capture prey, and are expert farmers. Terms like bird brain and monkey business take on a whole new meaning. Now, more Nature's Engineers on Modern Marvels. For centuries, we've often reflected that our superior intelligence has given rise to an exclusive ability to perform tasks by creating and using tools. What's brought us to the pinnacle and put us on the top of the food chain is our ability to create things with which we modify the environment. So if you need to make a hole, you know, we invented the drill. If you needed to get a nail into a piece of wood, first it was a rock and then it turned into a hammer. But it turns out that we're not the only tool users with the advantage of hands, with the dexterity made possible by an opposable thumb, and big brains. These are traits we share with the great apes. For centuries, the great apes were lumped in with monkeys and thought of as primitive and often frightening. They could also be amusing if they were tamed and trained to mimic people. Not so long ago, human perception of chimpanzees were these were just silly animals for our entertainment. And you would see that in television shows and movies. They'd be dressed up in clothes, doing tricks even at zoos. Like a parrot trained to talk, any skills they might happen to demonstrate would be attributed to their human role models. Monkey C, Monkey D. Fortunately, nowadays, our perception of them has changed. In 1960, Jane Goodall began the longest-running research project of wild chimpanzees. She discovered that they actually fashion, modify, and use tools. This actually made us, humans, redefine our uniqueness.

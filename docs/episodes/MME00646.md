European explorers to America came upon the bird in the 1500s and mistakenly identified it as a type of guinea fowl called a turkey cock, which got its name after it was imported to Europe via turkey. The cock was eventually dropped, and the turkey had a name that stuck. Whether or not it graced the pilgrim's first Thanksgiving is in dispute, but it's certain that Benjamin Franklin lobbied for it to become America's Franklin lobbied for it to become America's national bird. Still, turkeys have never been accused of being the brightest animals in the barn. After all, when frightened, domesticated turkeys will often bunch up in piles and suffocate one another. But there's one old story about turkey intelligence that's probably a myth. I've always heard the story told where a turkey, he'll just stick his head up when it rains and drowns. I've never seen that, 23 years of growing turkeys. If it starts raining, they come back in the house just like me and you would. The primary breed raised domestically is called the Nicholas White. All right. This is a hen, this is a tom. They're both the exact same age. Notable differences seen in these two sexes are the small, smooth profile of the hen head and a much smaller snood. That's right, a snood. And that fleshy thing hanging below the neck? That's a wattle. To turkeys, they're sexy. The wattle at the base of the neck, the snood, the different head coloration, all used to attract females for breeding in the wild. The eyes are offset on the side of the head to avoid predation."*
